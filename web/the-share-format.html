The share format

A chela share is nothing but a short header and a list of ordinary BIP-39 words - the same words a wallet seed uses. Everything needed to recover lives in those words; the header is a human-readable summary of what the words already say. This page explains what each word carries. It sits between the theory (why a handful of points rebuilds a secret) and the exact, byte-level SPEC.md (enough to write a compatible implementation).

The header line

Each share starts with a dashed code like CHELA-013A-21-2-3-25. It is a convenience, not a secret, and it only repeats what the words encode: 

CHELA - 013A - 21 - 2 - 3 - 25
        │      │    │   │   │
        │      │    │   │   └ word count
        │      │    │   └ total shares made (N)
        │      │    └ shares needed to recover (M)
        │      └ this share's coordinate (x)
        └ recovery-set id (which split this share belongs to)

Because the same facts are inside the words, a share recovers even if the header is smudged or torn off - chela reads the words and re-derives all of it.

What the words encode

Each word is 11 bits (one of 2048 wordlist entries). The words lay out in four parts: 

word 0          which share this is (x) and how many are needed (M)
word 1          the recovery-set id (a random per-split nonce)
words 2 … W-2   this share's piece of the secret (the body)
word W-1        a checksum that catches transcription errors

Word 0: x and M

The first word packs this share's coordinate x and the threshold M. They are stored as offsets (x-1 and M-2) so an impossible value literally cannot be written down. chela draws each x at random from 1 to 32 rather than handing out 1, 2, 3 in order, so a stray share gives away neither how many shares exist nor where it sits.

Word 1: the recovery-set id

Word 1 is a random 11-bit value drawn once per split and written identically into every share of that split - a batch stamp. It lets recovery confirm that the shares in front of it belong together and refuse a mix of two unrelated splits. It is not derived from the secret, so it reveals nothing about a low-entropy payload, and a fresh split draws a fresh id.

The body words: the secret, split

The middle words carry this share's Y values - its points on the curves from the theory. chela uses one curve per byte of the secret, all sharing the same x coordinates, with the arithmetic done over GF(2⁸) (a 256-element field, one byte at a time, no carries between bytes). On their own the body words look random; only a threshold of shares, combined, mean anything.

Where the secret ends, and what it is

What actually gets split is not the bare secret but a small body: the secret, then a one-byte integrity tag, then a one-byte kind byte. The kind byte records the payload type (a 12/15/18/21/24-word seed, with or without a passphrase, or free text) and doubles as a terminator that marks where the secret ends. The integrity tag is the first byte of SHA-256(secret ‖ kind); at recovery, a rebuilt body whose tag does not match is rejected, so the wrong set of shares is caught instead of returning a plausible-looking wrong secret.

The last word: the checksum

The final word is an 11-bit CRC-11/UMTS checksum over the share's decoded values - its x, M, the recovery-set id, and its body bytes. A mistyped word changes at most 11 bits, and an 11-bit CRC catches every burst error that short, so recovery rejects a corrupted share up front rather than feeding bad bytes into the math.

How recovery rebuilds the secret

Given any M shares, chela decodes and checksum-verifies each one, confirms they share one recovery-set id and threshold, then runs Lagrange interpolation at x = 0 - independently for each byte - to recover the body. It reads the kind byte to learn the payload type and where it ends, verifies the integrity tag, and only then hands back the secret. Fewer than M shares leave the body information-theoretically undetermined; there is nothing to brute-force.
